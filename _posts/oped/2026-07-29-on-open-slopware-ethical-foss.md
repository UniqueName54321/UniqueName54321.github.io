Hi! I'm a growing FOSS developer and software engineer - both metaphorically and, considering I'm still sixteen, probably literally too.

You might have seen some of my older projects floating around under the username `UniqueName12345`. My current GitHub account is [UniqueName54321](https://github.com/UniqueName54321), though. Most of the stuff on my old account was made while I was still figuring out what the hell I was doing, and it really does not represent the standards I hold myself to now.

At the moment, I'm working on two considerably more ambitious projects called **Jaboticaba** and **LycaraOS**. I'm not going to explain too much about either of them yet - no spoilers :P - but they are probably my biggest projects so far.

Anyway, recently I became aware of a project hosted on Codeberg called **Ethical FOSS**.

Based on the name, I initially assumed it would be about making FOSS communities more ethical: improving accessibility, discouraging abusive maintainers, protecting contributors, promoting privacy, making projects safer and more welcoming, that sort of thing.

And, to be fair, they do touch on some of that through their code of conduct.

However, that does not appear to be their main focus.

Instead, a rather large amount of the project is dedicated to generative AI - more specifically, arguing that its presence in or around a FOSS project is inherently suspicious, unethical or worthy of public labelling.

One of Ethical FOSS's projects is called **Open Slopware**, which is essentially a public list of open-source projects that have committed some variety of AI-related sin. These range from being mostly generated by an AI agent all the way down to merely permitting AI-assisted contributions.

So, yes: perhaps **Unethical POS** would have been a more accurate name.

Full disclosure before I continue: I absolutely have a horse in this race.

An artificial horse, admittedly, but still a horse.

I would not be remotely close to where I am today without AI. It has helped me learn programming, understand unfamiliar code, design systems, debug projects and turn ideas that previously existed only inside my head into actual working software.

That does not mean I think AI is perfect. It is very much *not* perfect. AI-generated code can be insecure, incorrect, bloated, unmaintainable or just impressively stupid (GRAPE, basically). Anyone using it without understanding or reviewing what it produces is essentially playing Russian roulette with a keyboard.

But I have also watched the technology evolve for years, used it extensively and developed my own standards for how it should - and should not - be involved in software development.

So no, I am not an unbiased observer.

Neither are the people maintaining Ethical FOSS.

## Problem 1: "Open Slopware" is, itself, kinda slop

Let's address the extremely AI-generated elephant in the room.

**Open Slopware is basically a giant public list of open-source projects that have committed some form of AI-related thoughtcrime.**

Some of the projects on it genuinely do appear to be heavily or carelessly generated. That is a reasonable thing to criticise! If somebody asks an AI agent to generate an entire application, barely reads the result and then releases it to actual human beings, there is a very real chance that the code is insecure, broken or held together by the software equivalent of wet cardboard.

The problem is that Open Slopware does not stop there.

It also targets projects for merely permitting AI-assisted contributions, including optional AI features, using AI to review code, accepting sponsorship from an AI company or simply declining to adopt an explicitly anti-AI position.

In other words, it takes several completely different questions - 

* Is this software badly generated?
* Does this project permit AI assistance?
* Does the application contain an AI feature?
* Does it use an external AI service?
* Does an AI company sponsor it?
* Do its maintainers hold the Correct Opinion™ about AI?

 - and bundles all of them together beneath the word **"slopware."**

That is not a particularly useful way to evaluate software.

It is also a rather sticky list. [Their own FAQ](https://codeberg.org/ethical-foss/open-slopware/src/branch/main/FAQ.md#why-is-removal-harder-than-landing-on-the-list-in-the-first-place) openly acknowledges that getting removed is harder than getting added.

In practice, this means a project can be listed based on evidence of AI involvement, but escaping the list may require a much broader change in policy or public position. At that point, it starts looking less like a neutral record of how software was developed and more like an ideological naughty list.

Open Slopware expresses these judgements through a collection of colourful badges, because apparently public shaming becomes more rigorous when it has graphic design.

### "Vibecoded"

This badge has a red background and is applied to projects that are supposedly written fully or mostly by AI - even when the resulting code has received human review.

This is the one category where I can understand the underlying concern.

AI agents are perfectly capable of generating insecure, incorrect or completely nonsensical code while presenting it with the confidence of someone who has never been wrong in their life. Tools that allow inexperienced users to generate and deploy entire applications can make it extremely easy to create security vulnerabilities without even realising it.

However, "written mostly by AI" still does not tell us enough by itself.

There is an enormous difference between:

* blindly publishing whatever an AI agent happens to push out;
* giving the output a five-second glance before merging it;
* and having a competent developer properly inspect, test, rewrite and take responsibility for it.

Human review is not *automatically* meaningful, but neither is it automatically meaning*less*.

So yes: criticising irresponsible vibecoding is fair. Unfortunately, this is where my praise ends.

### "Permissive AI Policy"

This orange badge is for projects that merely **allow** AI-assisted contributions.

Evidence can apparently include an `AI_POLICY.md`, a `CLAUDE.md` or code that the Open Slopware maintainers believe was produced using an LLM being merged into the main branch.

This does not:
* establish that a project is low quality.
* establish that contributions are unreviewed.
* establish that the maintainers do not understand their own code.

It establishes only that the project has not completely prohibited a particular category of development tool.

A project saying, "AI-assisted contributions are allowed, but they must meet the same review and quality requirements as everything else," is not even *remotely* equivalent to somebody pressing **GENERATE APP** and immediately deploying the result to production.

Treating those as variations of the same offence makes the label ridiculous and borderline useless.

### "AI Functionality"

This yellow badge applies to software that actually contains some form of AI functionality, such as an "Ask AI" feature - or, presumably, any of the many open-source applications whose entire purpose is running AI models locally.

This category is *especially* bizarre because it says almost nothing about how the software itself was developed.

A local model runner could be written entirely by humans, thoroughly tested, privacy-respecting *and* extremely useful. It would still receive an Open Slopware badge because the software performs AI-related tasks.

That is not a measurement of code quality.

It is a measurement of whether the maintainers approve of what the program does.

You are free to dislike AI software, obviously. Nobody is legally required to enjoy Ollama. But calling every FOSS application with AI functionality "slopware" is like calling Blender bad software because you dislike 3D animation.

Those are different arguments.

### "AI Code Reviews"

This blue badge is assigned when a project uses an AI tool to review pull requests.

Again, this tells us almost nothing by itself.

An AI review could be:
* the only review a pull request receives;
* an optional second opinion;
* an automated way to catch obvious mistakes before a human looks at it;
* or a glorified linting assistant that leaves comments nobody is required to obey.

Those arrangements are not equivalent.

Using an AI reviewer as an infallible replacement for maintainers would be reckless. Using one as an additional tool alongside human review is considerably less dramatic.

A bot leaving a comment on a pull request does not cause the Linux kernel to collapse into dust.

### "Gen AI 'Art'"

This badge uses a green background and the title **"Gen AI 'Art'"** - quotation marks included, because apparently the badge itself needed to summon the Discourse Machine.

There are genuine ethical, legal and artistic debates surrounding generated imagery. Questions about training data, artist consent, attribution, replacement of paid creative work and disclosure are all worth discussing.

However, those questions are largely separate from whether the underlying software is secure, maintainable or useful.

A project using a generated banner image does not mean its source code is AI-generated. A project using entirely human-made artwork does not mean its code is good.

Once again, Open Slopware is mixing together unrelated kinds of criticism and presenting them as though they all demonstrate the same defect.

### "AI Sponsored" and "AI Databroker Usage"

Open Slopware technically treats these as two separate badges, but I am grouping them together because both concern a project's relationship with an AI company rather than the quality of its code.

**"AI Sponsored"** means that an AI company financially supports the project.

**"AI Databroker Usage"** means that an AI-related company is listed as a third-party service involved in processing user data.

The second category could contain genuinely useful privacy information. Users should know where their data goes, which external services process it and whether prompts, code or personal information are retained.

But why limit the concern to AI companies?

A project sending user data to a non-AI analytics company, advertising platform or cloud provider can raise exactly the same - or worse - privacy issues.

The relevant question should be:

> What information is being sent, to whom, for what purpose, under what terms and with what degree of user consent?

Instead, the badge system appears to place most of its emphasis on whether the third party has the letters **A** and **I** somewhere in its business model.

Likewise, sponsorship does not prove that a project is badly made or controlled by its sponsor. (It may create a conflict of interest worth disclosing, but that is not unique to AI companies either)

Funding is not a code-quality metric.

### "AI in Issue Tracker"

This badge applies when a project uses an AI bot or agent to respond to issues, discussions, forums or other support channels.

There are plenty of bad ways to do this, but also plenty of good ways to do this.

An automated bot that closes valid bug reports, invents nonexistent fixes or traps users inside an endless loop of useless responses would be infuriating. Nobody enjoys being told, "Thank you for your feedback!" by a machine that has clearly not understood a single word.

But an assistant that labels issues, identifies duplicates, summarises long discussions or suggests relevant documentation could be genuinely useful - especially for small projects whose maintainers have limited time.

Once again, the implementation matters.

Open Slopware's badge tells us that AI is present. It does not tell us whether that presence is helpful, optional, supervised or catastrophically annoying.

Also, this definition technically means that any of the 2,668,000+ projects that use Dependabot technically count towards this badge.

### "Condones LLM Ingestion"

This black badge is awarded when a project supposedly "condones the use of AI/LLMs to ingest the project's contents."

That phrasing makes it sound like somebody is feeding the entire repository into a gigantic industrial content furnace.

In practice, it can apparently include something as simple as a project telling users:

> "If you do not understand part of the documentation or source code, you can ask ChatGPT to explain it."

There is a massive difference between:

* an individual user submitting a small section of code to an assistant;
* a project providing an optional documentation chatbot;
* a service indexing the repository for search;
* and a company scraping millions of repositories to train a commercial model.

Lumping all of these together as **"LLM ingestion"** strips away every distinction that actually matters: scale, consent, licensing, privacy and purpose.

It also creates a strange expectation that FOSS maintainers should police how users analyse publicly available source code on their own computers.

### "Anti-AI Policy"

Finally, we reach the green badge with a star: **"Anti-AI Policy."**

This is the "good" badge.

Projects receive it for explicitly opposing or prohibiting AI involvement.

And this is where the purpose of the list becomes especially obvious.

The system is not *merely* trying to document risks. It is not simply *warning* users about insecure generated code, opaque data processing or poorly supervised automation.

It also *rewards* projects for expressing the maintainers' preferred political and ethical position.

That transforms Open Slopware from a software-quality resource into an ideological scoring system.

Some of its badges describe potential technical risks. Some describe product functionality. Some describe funding. Some describe privacy practices. Some describe development policies. One exists primarily to award a gold star for agreeing with the people running the list.

These categories are not exclusive, they are not equally serious and several of them have almost no connection to whether the software is actually *slop*.

The result is a classification system where a dangerously vibecoded application, a carefully reviewed AI-assisted project and a fully human-written local model runner can all be placed beneath the same insulting label.

That does not help users make informed decisions.

It mostly tells them which projects have failed Ethical FOSS's ideological purity test.

## The Projects Themselves

So, what kind of software actually ends up on this list?

Pretty much everything.

Open Slopware includes projects ranging from tiny niche tools that maybe a thousand people use, all the way up to software that forms part of the basic plumbing of modern computing.

For example:

* **Kodi**, because of its permissive AI policy and its `Kodiai` project;
* **VLC**, for similar reasons, along with its newer AI-powered subtitle functionality;
* **Audacity**, over much of the same;
* **FFmpeg**, because of its permissive AI policies and, partially, [a now-deleted social-media post that used generated imagery](https://web.archive.org/web/20260513222612/https://twitter.com/FFmpeg/status/2054689695532036382);
* and, somehow, **the entire Linux kernel**.

Yes. The Linux kernel.

At a certain point, a list of supposedly unacceptable software stops being particularly useful when avoiding everything on it would require you to abandon half of modern computing and go live inside a cave with a mechanical typewriter.

FFmpeg is an especially funny example.

FFmpeg is not merely some random video converter you found on GitHub with twelve stars and a README written entirely in lowercase. It is one of the most widely used multimedia frameworks in existence and provides major parts of the underlying video-processing infrastructure used across the internet.

Sites and services such as YouTube depend heavily on technology built around it. It has been developed, tested and deployed in real-world production environments for decades.

You are, of course, free to dislike FFmpeg's stance on AI-assisted contributions. You can also object to a social-media post using generated artwork.

But none of that changes the fact that FFmpeg remains an extremely mature and battle-tested piece of software.

A badge does not make that technical reality disappear.

### Godot's Alleged AI Problem

Godot—the game engine I personally use—also receives the **"Permissive AI Policy"** badge.

Naturally, I checked the evidence.

It turns out that one of the cited examples was [a single pull request](https://github.com/godotengine/godot/pull/112471#issuecomment-4047941693) involving a glow-related visual-compatibility change. According to the discussion, the maintainers only discovered that Claude had authored it after the pull request had already been reviewed and merged.

That is apparently enough for Godot to receive an AI-policy warning badge.

Now, I could react to this by immediately abandoning one of the largest and most established open-source game engines available.

I could migrate all my projects to something called the **Pandemonium Game Engine**, which Open Slopware presents as an alternative and which, after a brief look, appears to be maintained almost entirely by one person.

Or I could continue using the mature engine with a large community, extensive documentation, years of development and an ecosystem I already understand.

I think I will choose option two.

Migrating an entire game-development workflow over one merged glow-compatibility pull request would be a Certified Bad Idea™.

This is where the practical limits of the list become obvious.

There are stricter anti-AI alternatives to projects such as Kodi, VLC and Audacity. Some users may genuinely prefer those alternatives, and that is completely fine.

But replacing software is not simply a matter of locating another program in the same category.

You also have to consider:

* stability;
* security;
* platform support;
* accessibility;
* documentation;
* plugin and extension ecosystems;
* file compatibility;
* maintenance capacity;
* and whether the alternative will still exist three years from now.

A smaller project with the "correct" AI policy is not automatically a safer, better or more ethical choice than a mature project with a permissive one.

That becomes even more obvious with infrastructure such as FFmpeg or the Linux kernel.

Regardless of anyone's opinions about generative AI, FFmpeg will continue powering a massive portion of the world's multimedia stack because it has been battle-tested for literal decades.

And the Linux kernel is, well, the Linux kernel.

At that scale, "just use an alternative" stops being practical advice and begins approaching performance art.

## The Accidental Recommendation System

There is another, considerably funnier problem with Open Slopware.

Because the repository categorises projects by exactly how they interact with AI, it can accidentally function as a tongue-in-cheek recommendation system.

Want software with no AI involvement whatsoever? Look for projects with the anti-AI badge.

Want software that allows AI-assisted contributions but is not mostly generated? Look for the permissive-policy badge.

Want applications that actually include AI functionality? The list has helpfully categorised those too.

It is less an effective boycott tool and more:

> "Choose your preferred level of AI involvement, and we will show you some software."

This genuinely happened to me with a program called [`brush`](https://github.com/reubeno/brush).

Under the **Command-Line Shells** category, I noticed `brush`, which had been tagged for generated artwork used in its README.

I had never heard of it before, so naturally I clicked on it to see what horrific AI-infested slop I was apparently supposed to avoid.

Unfortunately for Open Slopware, it looked pretty good.

`brush` is a Bash-compatible shell written in Rust, and after looking through the project, I came away thinking it might actually be a solid Bash alternative—or at least interesting enough that I may switch to it whenever I return to using Linux regularly.

So the blacklist directly introduced me to software I might eventually adopt.

Absolutely devastating boycott. No notes.

This is what happens when the criteria for inclusion are broad enough that they stop separating genuinely irresponsible software from ordinary, useful projects that happen to have touched AI somewhere in their development, branding or surrounding community.

Instead of warning people away, the list can become a searchable directory of:

> "Open-source software that did something involving AI at some point."

Which, to be fair, is quite a useful resource.

Just probably not in the way its maintainers intended.
